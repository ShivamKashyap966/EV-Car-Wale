Where Does Electricity Come From?

A deep dive into India's energy generation mix, grid emissions, and what it means for your EV's true environmental impact.

6 min read EV Car Wale Team Updated July 2026

Every time you plug in your EV, you're drawing power from India's vast electricity grid — a complex network of power plants, transmission lines, and distribution systems. But where does that electricity actually come from? Understanding India's energy mix helps you grasp the true environmental impact of driving an electric vehicle, from the power plant to the wheel.

India is the third-largest electricity producer in the world, with a total installed capacity exceeding 430 GW as of 2026. The generation mix is a diverse range of sources, each contributing to the grid in different proportions:

India's total installed capacity includes both utility-scale and grid-connected renewable sources.

Coal remains the dominant source, though its share has been declining steadily from 65% in 2020 to 55% in 2025 as renewable capacity expands. The government has set a target of 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030.

Notably, solar has overtaken hydroelectricity as the third-largest source, driven by massive installations in Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Karnataka. India now ranks fifth globally in installed solar capacity.

Grid Emission Factors

The grid emission factor (GEF) measures how many grams of CO₂ are emitted for every kilowatt-hour (kWh) of electricity consumed. India's grid emission factor is approximately 650 gCO₂/kWh — one of the highest in the world due to heavy coal dependence.

To put this in perspective: charging a 40 kWh EV battery emits about 26 kg of CO₂ at the grid level. An equivalent petrol car (20 km/l) covering 300 km would emit approximately 52 kg of CO₂. So even on India's coal-heavy grid, an EV produces roughly half the CO₂ emissions of a petrol car.

How EV Charging Impacts the Grid

A single EV drawing 7.2 kW from a home charger consumes roughly as much power as 5–6 households running simultaneously. When millions of EVs charge at the same time — especially during peak evening hours — grid stress becomes a legitimate concern.

However, studies show that with smart charging and time-of-day tariffs, the grid can easily handle mass EV adoption. Most EV charging happens overnight (11 PM–6 AM) when grid demand is at its lowest, effectively utilising idle generation capacity.

The Indian government and power utilities are implementing V2G (Vehicle-to-Grid) pilot projects, where EVs act as mobile batteries that can discharge power back to the grid when demand peaks — turning the potential challenge into an opportunity.

Renewable Energy Certificates & Green Charging

RECs (Renewable Energy Certificates) allow EV owners and businesses to offset their electricity consumption with renewable energy, even if their local grid is fossil-fuel-heavy. One REC represents 1 MWh of renewable energy fed into the grid.

Several Indian charging networks now offer green charging options. For example, Tata Power EV charging stations are powered by renewable energy through REC purchases, and ChargeZone has committed to 100% renewable energy for its chargers by 2027.

Individual EV owners can also purchase RECs to offset their home charging emissions. At roughly ₹500–800 per REC (1,000 kWh), offsetting a full year of EV driving (say 6,000 kWh) costs just ₹3,000–4,800 — making your EV effectively net-zero on emissions.

Time-of-Day Tariffs for EV Charging

Many Indian states have introduced time-of-day (ToD) tariffs specifically for EV charging. These tariffs offer lower per-unit rates during off-peak hours to incentivise charging when grid demand is low and renewable generation (especially wind) is high.

  • • Off-peak (11 PM – 6 AM): ₹4–5/kWh — cheapest rates, ideal for overnight charging
  • • Shoulder (6 AM – 6 PM): ₹6–8/kWh — moderate rates
  • • Peak (6 PM – 11 PM): ₹9–12/kWh — highest rates, avoid if possible

By scheduling your EV to charge during off-peak hours, you can save 40–50% on charging costs while also reducing strain on the grid during peak demand periods. Most modern EVs and chargers support scheduled charging — set it once and forget it.

Solar-Powered Home Charging

Installing rooftop solar panels is the ultimate way to charge your EV with 100% clean energy. A typical 5 kW solar system can generate 500–700 kWh per month — enough to power both a home and an EV driving 1,200–1,500 km per month.

With the Indian government's PM-Surya Ghar scheme offering subsidies of up to ₹78,000 for rooftop solar installation, the upfront cost has dropped significantly. The payback period for a solar + EV charging system is typically 3–5 years, depending on your state's net metering policy.

During daylight hours, your solar panels feed power directly into your home and EV charger. Any excess is exported to the grid via net metering, earning you credits. At night, you draw from the grid (or from a home battery if installed).

☀

Solar + EV: The Perfect Pair

A home with a 5 kW solar system and an EV can reduce its carbon footprint by 3–4 tonnes of CO₂ per year compared to a petrol-car household. With net metering, your electricity bill can drop to near zero — making both your home and car run on sunshine.

Well-to-Wheel Emissions: The Full Picture

Well-to-wheel analysis accounts for every stage of the energy lifecycle — from extraction (well) to consumption (wheel). For EVs, this includes:

  • 1. Fuel cycle: Coal mining, gas extraction, uranium mining, solar panel manufacturing, wind turbine construction
  • 2. Power generation: Burning fuel or converting renewable energy at power plants
  • 3. Transmission & distribution: Line losses (India averages 15–20% T&D losses)
  • 4. Charging & driving: Battery efficiency, charger losses, and motor efficiency

Even accounting for all these factors, EVs on India's grid emit 40–55% less CO₂ than petrol cars and 30–40% less than diesel cars on a well-to-wheel basis. As the grid gets cleaner — with the government targeting 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030 — the environmental advantage of EVs will only improve.

Conclusion

Electricity powers our modern world, and understanding where it comes from is key to making informed decisions about EV ownership. While India's grid still relies heavily on coal, the rapid growth of renewable energy — particularly solar — is rapidly changing the picture.

Every EV sold in India today is already significantly cleaner than its petrol counterpart, even on the current grid. By choosing green charging options, installing rooftop solar, or simply charging during off-peak hours, you can make your EV even more sustainable.

The future is electric, and the grid is getting greener every year. The question isn't whether India's electricity is clean enough for EVs — it's how quickly we can build more renewable capacity to power the electric mobility revolution.
