EV Infrastructure in India

An in-depth look at India's rapidly growing EV charging network, government targets, state-level progress, and the challenges that lie ahead.

8 MIN READ INFRASTRUCTURE

India's electric vehicle revolution hinges on one critical factor — charging infrastructure. While EV sales have surged over the past three years, the charging network has struggled to keep pace. As of early 2026, India has deployed over 12,000 public charging stations with approximately 25,000 charging points across the country. Yet, with a vehicle-to-charger ratio hovering around 30:1 — far behind the global benchmark of 10:1 — there is significant ground to cover.

India's public charging network is heavily concentrated in a handful of states. Maharashtra, Delhi NCR, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Gujarat account for more than 65% of all public charging stations. Tier-1 cities — Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Pune — dominate the deployment map, while tier-2 and tier-3 cities remain significantly underserved.

The government's target, set under the National Electric Mobility Mission Plan (NEMMP) and reinforced by the Ministry of Power's 2022 guidelines, calls for one public charging station every 3 km in cities and every 25 km on highways. As of early 2026, Delhi is the only state that has come close to meeting the 3-km target, with charging stations available at nearly every 3.5 km radius within the city limits.

The types of chargers deployed are also evolving. While CCS2 has emerged as the dominant standard for DC fast charging, a significant number of CHAdeMO chargers remain operational, particularly from the early deployment phase. AC Level 2 chargers (Type 2) are common in semi-public spaces like malls, offices, and apartment complexes.

DC fast chargers are concentrated along major highway corridors (Delhi-Mumbai, Delhi-Jaipur, Mumbai-Pune, Bengaluru-Chennai) and within city limits. AC chargers dominate in workplace and destination charging scenarios, while battery swapping — led by Bounce and Sun Mobility — has gained traction for two-wheelers and three-wheelers in urban logistics.

Delhi leads in charging density (stations per square kilometre) thanks to its smaller geographic area and aggressive policy push. Maharashtra leads in absolute numbers, driven by Mumbai, Pune, and the Mumbai-Pune expressway corridor. Southern states collectively have higher deployment rates than the north, with Karnataka and Tamil Nadu emerging as early adopters of public charging infrastructure.

04

Government Targets

  • →1 station per 3 km in all million-plus cities by 2027
  • →1 station per 25 km on all national highways by 2027
  • →46,000 public charging stations targeted by 2030 under NEMMP
  • →100% electrification of all new government fleet vehicles by 2030
  • →Mandatory EV-ready building codes for all new residential and commercial constructions

The Ministry of Power has mandated that state electricity distribution companies (discoms) must facilitate EV charging infrastructure by offering separate EV tariffs, expediting meter installations, and ensuring grid readiness. The Ministry of Road Transport & Highways has additionally directed that all new highway fuel stations must include EV charging bays.

05

Key Challenges

Grid Load & Power Availability

A single DC fast charger (50 kW) draws as much power as 50 average Indian households. With thousands of such chargers planned, utilities must upgrade transformers, feeders, and substations. Many states, particularly in rural and semi-urban areas, already face power deficits, making grid reinforcement a multi-year challenge.

Land Acquisition & Real Estate

Setting up a charging station requires suitable land with adequate power connectivity — scarce in dense urban areas. High real estate costs in prime locations make it difficult for operators to achieve profitability. Many operators are now partnering with malls, hotels, and petrol pumps to lease existing spaces rather than acquiring new land.

Interoperability & Payment Fragmentation

Each charging network — Tata Power, ChargeZone, Statiq, Zeon, and others — operates its own mobile app, RFID card, and payment system. There is no universal roaming agreement, forcing EV owners to carry multiple apps and accounts. The lack of UPI-based seamless payment at all stations remains a friction point.

Maintenance & Uptime

Surveys indicate that up to 20-25% of public charging points in India are non-functional at any given time due to network issues, vandalism, or component failures. Unlike petrol pumps which have dedicated attendants, many EV charging stations are unattended, leading to delayed repairs and poor user experience.

06

Private Players Driving Growth

Several private companies are racing to build India's charging network, each with a different strategy:

Tata Power EV

India's largest CPO (Charge Point Operator) with over 3,000 public charging points across 400+ cities. Focused on DC fast chargers along highways and AC chargers at workplaces and residential complexes. Aggressively partnering with Tata Group hotels, Croma stores, and Indian Oil petrol pumps.

ChargeZone

India's largest private EV charging network with 2,500+ points. Strong presence in the western corridor (Mumbai-Ahmedabad-Delhi). Known for high-quality, well-maintained stations with canopies and lounge areas. Recently raised $50M in Series B funding for expansion into southern markets.

Statiq

Focuses on dense urban deployment with 1,800+ points in Delhi NCR, Bengaluru, and Pune. Uses a franchise model to install chargers at existing businesses. Known for competitive pricing and a user-friendly app with real-time availability data.

Zeon & Magenta

Zeon operates 1,000+ charging points with a focus on interoperability and open protocols. Magenta Power offers end-to-end solutions including ChargeGrid and home charger installations, with over 500 stations in the Mumbai-Pune belt.

07

Future Roadmap

The coming five years will be decisive for India's EV charging infrastructure. Key developments to watch:

  • •Ultra-fast (350 kW) charging hubs: Ionity-style charging corridors with 150-350 kW chargers every 50 km on the Golden Quadrilateral by 2028.
  • •Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) 2.0: Mandated interoperability to enable seamless roaming across networks, similar to the European Union's ISO 15118 standard.
  • •V2G (Vehicle-to-Grid) pilots: Tata Power and NITI Aayog planning V2G trials in Delhi and Mumbai, allowing EV batteries to feed power back to the grid during peak demand.
  • •Solar-integrated charging stations: Mandatory rooftop solar requirements for all new public charging stations, reducing grid dependency and operational costs.
  • •Battery swapping standardization: Government working on unified battery swapping standards for two/three-wheelers to accelerate adoption in the last-mile delivery segment.
KEY TAKEAWAYS
  • →India has crossed 12,000 public charging stations but needs 46,000 by 2030 to meet EV adoption targets.
  • →Charging infrastructure is heavily skewed toward 5-6 states; tier-2/3 cities remain underserved.
  • →Delhi is the only city approaching the 3-km station density target; most others are at 5-10 km intervals.
  • →Private players (Tata Power, ChargeZone, Statiq) are driving deployment faster than public sector entities.
  • →Interoperability, grid readiness, and maintenance uptime remain the top three challenges.
  • →The FAME III scheme and PLI for battery manufacturing will accelerate domestic charger production and deployment.
08

Conclusion

India's EV charging infrastructure is at an inflection point. The groundwork has been laid — policy frameworks are in place, private capital is flowing, and early adopters have demonstrated that the model works. However, the transition from early adoption to mass market will require a step-change in deployment speed, reliability, and geographic coverage.

The next 24 months will be critical. With FAME III expected to allocate significant funds specifically for public charging infrastructure, combined with state-level mandates and private sector competition, India has a realistic path to achieving its 2030 targets. For EV buyers, the charging network is no longer a deal-breaker — but it remains a factor that requires planning, especially for inter-city travel.

As one industry executive put it: "Two years ago, range anxiety was the biggest barrier. Today, it's charging anxiety — but that gap is closing fast."
